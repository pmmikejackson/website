**Every week, someone tells me AI is coming for my job. Every other week, someone tells me AI just made me unstoppable.**

They can't both be right. I don't think either of them is.

Pick any product feed and you'll find the two camps. The first says AI will replace product managers. It writes the PRD, drafts the user stories, builds the roadmap deck, and summarizes the customer interviews. Why pay a person to do that?

The second camp says the opposite. AI turns every PM into a one-person product team. Spec in the morning, working prototype by lunch, launch by Friday. Who needs engineers, designers, or researchers when you have a chat window?

I've spent 15+ years in restaurant technology, from installing POS systems to running international product teams. I use these tools every day. And I keep coming back to the same conclusion.

Wrong question.

## Both Camps Make the Same Mistake

Look closely and both arguments rest on the same assumption: that the product manager's job is producing artifacts.

PRDs. User stories. Roadmaps. Mockups. Status reports.

If that's the job, then the pessimists have a point. AI is very good at artifacts. It will produce a clean, well-formatted, plausible PRD faster than I can open a blank document.

And if that's the job, the optimists have a point too. A PM who can generate artifacts ten times faster looks ten times more productive.

But that was never the job. The artifacts were the receipt for the work, not the work.

## What the Job Actually Was

When I think about a large POS rollout across multiple countries, the requirements document was the easy part. The hard part was everything around it.

Which market goes first, and why. Which local requirement wins when two of them conflict. Who has to agree before anything ships. What we tell a restaurant when an update fails during the dinner rush. Which feature request we say no to, and how we say no so the relationship survives.

None of that lives in a document. It lives in judgment, context, and trust that took years to build.

AI didn't change any of that. What it changed is how much the receipt costs.

## What Actually Changes

When an artifact costs almost nothing to produce, three things shift.

**Volume stops meaning progress.** A team that generates five specs a week isn't five times more productive than a team that writes one. It may just have five times more to read. The pile of documents used to be a rough signal that thinking had happened. That signal is gone.

**The bottleneck moves to deciding.** If a prototype takes an afternoon instead of a month, the slow part is no longer building. It's knowing what to build, what to kill, and what to ignore. That's the part of the job that was always the PM's.

**Verifying becomes a skill.** AI output looks finished before it is. The PRD reads well. The prototype clicks through. The summary sounds confident. Somebody has to know the domain well enough to spot what's missing, and that somebody is usually the PM.

I learned that last one firsthand. I once used AI to go from idea to [working prototype in a weekend](/blog/from-napkins-to-live-code-20-years-of-restaurant-tech-pain-points). The tools were fast. They were also happy to help me build the wrong thing with total confidence. My starting assumption about the problem turned out to be wrong, and no amount of speed would have fixed that. Domain knowledge did.

## Productive, But Greasy

I want to be honest about where I land, because I don't think anyone has this figured out.

These tools are productive. I'm not going back.

They're also greasy. Hard to get a firm grip on. They slide from brilliant to wrong inside the same answer, and they never tell you which part is which. Use them long enough and you catch yourself confusing motion with progress, because the output arrives so fast it feels like work got done.

So I hold two things at once. AI is making me faster at parts of my job. And it's making it easier than ever to look busy while the real decisions sit untouched.

Anyone who tells you they're certain how this ends, in either direction, is selling something.

## The Right Question

Stop asking, "Will AI replace product managers?" And stop asking, "How much more can one PM do with AI?"

Ask this instead: **"Which part of my job was the artifact, and which part was the judgment?"**

Hand the artifacts to the machine. Let it draft, summarize, mock up, and format. Then spend the time you got back on the parts it can't touch: talking to customers, making the hard call, saying no, and checking the confident answer before it ships.

The PMs who get replaced will be the ones whose job really was the receipt. The PMs who think AI made them omnipotent will learn the hard way that a fast wrong answer is still wrong.

The rest of us have the same job we always had. We just have less excuse for not doing the hard part.
